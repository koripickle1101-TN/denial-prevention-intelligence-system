Denial Prevention Intelligence System

I built DPIS to study what may happen before a denial becomes visible. Instead of starting only with the denied claim, I use the project to ask what earlier eligibility, authorization, documentation-readiness, payer-requirement, ownership, or claim-readiness issue may have made that downstream problem possible.

As I work toward my Bachelor's of Science degree in Healthcare Administration, I use simulated projects like this one to practice revenue-cycle workflow analysis without presenting student work as professional healthcare employment, coding authority, payer authority, or real-world deployment.

Patient-to-Professional Perspective

A denial may look like a billing problem once it becomes visible, but patients experience the result rather than the internal workflow label. That can mean another phone call, a confusing bill, delayed resolution, uncertainty about coverage, or being pulled into an administrative problem they did not create.

01

Why I Built DPIS

I wanted to practice looking upstream instead of treating denial work as something that begins only after a claim has already failed.

The visible signal

A denial, rejection, claim problem, or payer response can be the point where an issue becomes visible.

The question behind it

Was there an earlier eligibility conflict, authorization-service alignment issue, documentation-readiness flag, payer-requirement issue, ownership gap, or other unresolved condition that should have been reviewed sooner?

What I am practicing

Separating the downstream signal from possible upstream conditions and documenting that reasoning without assuming every denial has the same cause.

Why it matters

Looking earlier in the workflow helps me see how administrative conditions can contribute to staff rework, revenue-cycle burden, and patient confusion without claiming that a student model can prove causation.

How I Think About Denial Prevention

I use DPIS to trace the workflow backward from the point where the problem becomes visible.

1. Eligibility + Intake

Was the fictional coverage and member information reviewed, and were obvious inconsistencies resolved?

2. Authorization Alignment

Were authorization requirements and service/date alignment reviewed in the simulation?

3. Documentation + Payer Requirements

Were documentation-readiness flags identified and relevant current payer requirements routed for appropriate review?

4. Claim Readiness

Was the fictional case ready to move downstream, or was an unresolved administrative issue still present?

5. Denial / Claim Signal

If a problem appears later, what earlier condition should be investigated rather than assumed?

Question I Keep Coming Back To

What upstream condition made this denial risk possible, and where could the workflow have been reviewed sooner?

04

What I Learned

A denial code or payer response may be a signal, but it is not automatically the full root cause.

Building DPIS pushed me to stop at the point where a denial becomes visible and then work backward. That means asking what information, requirement, ownership decision, or handoff existed earlier instead of assuming the downstream response tells the whole story.

That distinction matters to me because patients usually do not see the eligibility checks, authorization reviews, documentation requirements, claim edits, or handoffs behind the scenes. They experience the result. Learning to connect those two perspectives is part of how I am moving from lived patient experience toward healthcare operations work.

What DPIS Is — and Is Not

DPIS is a student-developed educational project using synthetic data. It has not been deployed in a healthcare organization and does not make coverage, coding, clinical, medical-necessity, compliance, or payer decisions. I do not claim that it has reduced real denials, increased reimbursement, improved productivity, or changed patient outcomes.

Healthcare coding, coverage, billing, and payer requirements can change. DPIS does not replace current payer policies, CMS guidance, coding resources, organizational procedures, or qualified professional review.
