Core operating rule

Clearinghouse accepted ≠ payer accepted.

A successful clearinghouse transmission proves that one checkpoint was passed. It does not, by itself, prove payer receipt, payer acknowledgement, entry into adjudication, or final resolution.

04

Claim-Control Decision Logic

The control does not treat a transmitted claim as completed work. It evaluates whether downstream payer visibility and ownership exist.

Claim not ready / transmission absent

Do not advance

Return to pre-submission readiness or transmission control rather than assuming the payer handoff began.

Clearinghouse rejected

Hold at transmission checkpoint

Review the clearinghouse response and route the correction before treating the claim as downstream.

Clearinghouse accepted / payer unknown

Handoff not proven

Confirm payer acknowledgement, payer reference, claim presence, and next-action ownership.

Payer rejection / claim not found

Route an exception

Identify the supported exception category, assign ownership, document evidence, and set follow-up.

Payer acknowledged / adjudication active

Actively moving — not closed

Maintain status visibility and follow-up until final disposition is documented.

Final status received

Verify downstream closure

Final status alone is not enough if a denial, rejection, correction, or other action still needs ownership.

Closure verified

Controlled final state

The modeled final status, required downstream action, evidence trail, and closure are documented.

Control rule

A successful clearinghouse transmission is not completed claim progression.

DPIS treats the claim as controlled only when downstream payer acknowledgement and status visibility are established, or when an exception has a visible owner, next action, follow-up timing, evidence trail, and eventual closure.

05

Patient-to-Professional Perspective

“Why hasn't my insurance processed this?”
“Why does the office say they sent it but insurance says they don't have it?”
“Why am I getting a bill already?”

The patient sees the delay. Healthcare operations has to determine where the claim actually stopped moving.

Where did the workflow first lose control? The downstream patient experience does not tell us whether the first break was readiness, transmission, clearinghouse response, payer acknowledgement, adjudication visibility, or follow-up. The workflow evidence does.

How this extends DPIS

Pre-submission readiness → transmission → payer handoff → downstream claim control

Pre-Submission Claim Readiness Control™ asks whether the modeled claim is ready to leave the upstream workflow. Claim Transmission & Acknowledgement Control™ then tests transmission and payer-side visibility. Clearinghouse-to-Payer Handoff Review™ specifically asks whether evidence exists that the claim entered payer-controlled processing.

When a downstream rejection or denial appears, the case can move into Denial Traceback Review™ to investigate where the workflow may have first lost control.

Source / evidence boundary

Operational concepts retained; unsupported performance claims excluded

This portfolio update uses the source's workflow concepts—claim validation, transmission, routing, status visibility, payer acknowledgement, and remittance-related context—but does not reuse the source's unsupported 95–98% clean-claim / first-pass acceptance figure or treat every described clearinghouse capability as universal.

Actual clearinghouse services, edits, transaction handling, payer connectivity, status reporting, and remittance workflows vary by vendor, payer, contract, implementation, and organization.

Educational boundary

What this control does not do

This simulation does not transmit real claims, access a clearinghouse or payer, reproduce live EDI transactions, determine payer acceptance, adjudicate claims, calculate reimbursement, interpret payer contracts, or make coding, coverage, medical-necessity, compliance, privacy/security, or legal determinations.
